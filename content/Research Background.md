For my PhD in Neuroscience at UNC Chapel Hill, completed in 2026 in the lab of Dr. Michael Love, I studied how genetic and social risk combine in bipolar disorder. My dissertation, *Genetic and Social Risk for Bipolar Disorder: Equity Gaps, Clinical Readiness, and the Responsible Use of Polygenic Scores*, examined what genetic risk scores can and cannot tell us about psychiatric risk.

Its central paper, [published in *Human Genetics and Genomics Advances*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xhgg.2026.100665) in 2026, analyzed 115,275 participants in the *All of Us* Research Program. Each of the six social risk factors I tested, from perceived stress to adverse childhood experiences, was more strongly associated with bipolar disorder than polygenic risk. People with the lowest genetic risk but a high social burden had rates of bipolar disorder comparable to or higher than people with the highest genetic risk and a low social burden. The work argues for models of psychiatric risk that weigh social context alongside genetics.

I also coordinated a shared analysis pipeline across five centers of the international Impact of Genomic Variation on Function (IGVF) Consortium. Before graduate school, as a bioinformatic analyst at the OU Health Sciences Center, I analyzed sequencing data for more than 20 labs across oncology, immunology, and metabolic disease.

My graduate research was supported by an NSF Graduate Research Fellowship and the UNC Department of Neuroscience T32 Training Grant.
